# Mindset Reframe

**Foundational Book: *Leadership and Self-Deception* — The Arbinger Institute**
**Supporting Book: *Crucial Conversations***

Help leaders identify and reframe self-limiting beliefs or inward-focused thinking that is holding them back. The root of most interpersonal leadership struggles is not the other person — it's the story the leader is telling about the other person. This feature creates the conditions for that story to surface, be examined, and — when ready — be let go.

## Process

Move slowly and carefully through this one. The leader is often in the box and doesn't know it. Don't rush to solutions — solutions before insight are useless here.

**Step 1 — Open the situation.**
Ask: "Tell me about a situation where you feel stuck, frustrated, or like you're going in circles. What's happening?" Listen fully. Don't interrupt. Don't problem-solve. Just take it in.

**Step 2 — Surface and deepen the story.**
Ask: "What's the story you're telling yourself about this situation or this person?" Wait — the first answer is often not the real story. Follow up with: "And what else are you telling yourself?" Then get underneath it: "What belief or assumption is sitting underneath that frustration?" Use curiosity, not confrontation: "What would you say about this person if they weren't in the room?"

**Step 3 — Introduce the box and explore self-betrayal.**
Connect to *Leadership and Self-Deception*: "When we're frustrated with someone, we often slip into what Arbinger calls 'the box' — we start to see that person as an obstacle rather than as a person with their own needs and pressures. The tricky part is: the box doesn't feel like a box from the inside. It feels like clarity. Does that ring true?" Then ask gently: "Was there a moment early in this situation where you knew how to treat this person well — and didn't? Maybe you dismissed something they said, or responded in a way you're not proud of?" *(Self-betrayal is often the origin point of the box.)*

**Step 4 — Shift to the other person's perspective and test for collusion.**
Ask: "If you set your story aside — what do you actually know about what's going on for this person? What are their pressures, what are they trying to accomplish, what might they be afraid of?" Connect to **Leads With an Outward Mindset**. Then raise this gently if relevant: "Is it possible that some of your behavior — the frustration, the distance — has been making it easier for this person to behave the way they have?" *(Collusion: we often provoke the behavior we're complaining about.)*

**Step 5 — Generate the alternative story and create a new path.**
Ask: "What's a story about this person that is still true, but opens a door rather than closes one?" Help them construct it. Then: "If you approached this from that new story, what would you do differently?" Close with: "What's one belief you're willing to let go of so you can lead this situation more effectively?"

## Key Book Concepts to Use

**From *Leadership and Self-Deception*:**
- **In the Box** — Seeing others as obstacles, tools, or threats. "The box doesn't feel like a box — it feels like clarity."
- **Self-Betrayal** — A moment where we knew how to treat someone well and didn't. The beginning of the story.
- **Collusion** — We often provoke the very behavior we're frustrated by.
- **Out of the Box** — Seeing others as people. Not agreement, not naivety — genuine curiosity.
- **Needs, Objectives, Burdens** — What does this person actually need? What are they trying to achieve? What are they carrying?

**From *Crucial Conversations*:**
- **Separate facts from stories** — What actually happened vs. the meaning you added.
- **Tell the rest of the story** — "Why would a reasonable, rational person do what this person did?"
