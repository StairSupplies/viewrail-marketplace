# Foundational Books for the Viewrail Leadership Performance Drivers

The six Leadership Performance Drivers are grounded in these six books. When coaching, draw on these frameworks explicitly — name the concepts, use the language, and reference the books when it will resonate with the leader.

---

## The Coaching Habit — Michael Bungay Stanier
**Drives: Develops People Through Coaching**

**Core premise:** The biggest obstacle to effective leadership is the Advice Monster — the compulsive urge to give advice, jump to solutions, and answer questions before the other person has had a chance to think. The antidote is staying curious a little longer and rushing to action a little more slowly.

**Key frameworks to use in coaching:**
- **The Advice Monster** — Name it directly when a leader is tempted to tell instead of ask. "That sounds like your Advice Monster talking."
- **The 7 Essential Questions:**
  1. *The Kickstart Question:* "What's on your mind?"
  2. *The AWE Question:* "And what else?" (most powerful coaching question — use it often)
  3. *The Focus Question:* "What's the real challenge here for you?"
  4. *The Foundation Question:* "What do you want?"
  5. *The Lazy Question:* "How can I help?"
  6. *The Strategic Question:* "If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"
  7. *The Learning Question:* "What was most useful for you?"
- **Create a Coaching Habit** — The shift from advice-giving to coaching is not one big move, it's building small habits across many moments.

**Coaching language to use:**
- "And what else?" after every initial answer
- "What's the real challenge here for you?" when a leader brings a surface-level problem
- "What do you want?" when someone is venting or unclear
- "How can I help?" instead of jumping to advice

## Leadership and Self-Deception — The Arbinger Institute
**Drives: Leads With an Outward Mindset**

**Core premise:** Most leadership problems start with self-deception — the moment we stop seeing others as full human beings and start seeing them as objects, obstacles, or vehicles for our own goals. This shift into the "box" (an inward mindset) distorts everything: our perception, our decisions, and our relationships. The shift out begins with seeing others as people.

**Key frameworks to use in coaching:**
- **In the Box vs. Out of the Box** — When a leader is in the box, they see people as obstacles or tools. When they're out of the box, they see people as people with their own needs and pressures.
  - Use: "It sounds like you might be in the box with this person right now. What's actually going on for them?"
- **Self-Betrayal** — Self-deception begins when we betray a feeling we have about how to treat someone, then justify it. The justification becomes a story we tell ourselves.
  - Use: "What's the story you're telling yourself about this person? Where might that story have started?"
- **Collusion** — When we're in the box, we often provoke others to behave badly, confirming our story about them.
  - Use: "Is it possible your behavior is inviting the response you're frustrated by?"
- **The Outward Mindset Shift** — Seeing others as people doesn't require agreement. It requires genuine curiosity about their experience.

**Coaching language to use:**
- "Are you seeing this person as a problem to solve, or as a person with their own pressures?"
- "What do you know about what's going on for them right now?"
- "What story are you telling yourself? Is it helping you or limiting you?"
- "What would it change if you assumed positive intent?"

## Crucial Conversations — Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan, Al Switzler
**Drives: Builds Trust & Psychological Safety**

**Core premise:** The conversations that matter most are the ones we avoid most. When stakes are high, opinions differ, and emotions run strong, most people either silence themselves or become aggressive — both of which destroy psychological safety and trust. Crucial conversations require a rare combination of candor and care.

**Key frameworks to use in coaching:**
- **Start with Heart** — Before any high-stakes conversation, get clear on what you really want. For yourself. For the other person. For the relationship.
  - Use: "What do you really want from this conversation? What do you want for this person? What do you want for the relationship?"
- **Make It Safe** — People shut down when they feel unsafe. Safety requires two things: mutual purpose (they believe you care about their goals) and mutual respect (they believe you respect them).
  - Use: "Have you signaled to this person that you're on their side? That you respect them?"
- **STATE Your Path** — Share your facts, Tell your story, Ask for their path, Talk tentatively, Encourage testing.
  - Use in conversation prep: "What are the facts? What story are you adding to those facts? Have you separated those two things?"
- **The Silence-to-Violence Spectrum** — Under pressure, people either go silent (mask, avoid, withdraw) or violent (control, label, attack). Neither works.
  - Use: "When this conversation gets uncomfortable, which direction do you tend to go — silence or pushing harder?"
- **Pool of Shared Meaning** — The goal of a crucial conversation is not to win but to fill the shared pool of meaning so everyone has the same information to make a decision.

**Coaching language to use:**
- "What do you really want from this conversation — for yourself, for them, for the relationship?"
- "Have you created safety before you go for candor?"
- "Are you sharing facts, or are you sharing your story about the facts?"
- "What would it look like to be both honest and caring at the same time?"

## Turn the Ship Around! — L. David Marquet
**Drives: Empowers Through Ownership**

**Core premise:** Traditional leadership is Leader-Follower: the leader gives orders and the team executes. This creates compliance but kills engagement, ownership, and initiative. Marquet transformed the USS Santa Fe by shifting to a Leader-Leader model — pushing authority down to where the information was, and developing competence and clarity throughout the team.

**Key frameworks to use in coaching:**
- **"I intend to..." instead of "Request permission to..."** — The most powerful shift. Instead of asking for approval, team members state their intentions. This forces clarity, builds accountability, and develops judgment.
  - Use: "Are your team members coming to you for answers, or are they coming with intentions? What would it take to shift that?"
- **Leader-Leader vs. Leader-Follower** — Describe what these two models look like in practice. Leader-Follower: "What should I do?" Leader-Leader: "Here's what I'm going to do."
  - Use: "Right now, is your team operating as leaders or as followers? What role are you playing in that?"
- **Control, Competence, Clarity** — You can push control down only when people have the competence to handle it and the clarity to know where they're going.
  - Use: "Before you give your team more ownership, are they clear on the goal? Do they have the skills to execute? What's missing?"
- **Resist the pull to give solutions** — Every time a leader answers a question that the team could answer themselves, they take back control.
  - Use: "What would happen if you said, 'What do you intend to do?' instead of answering their question?"

**Coaching language to use:**
- "What would it look like for your team to state intentions instead of asking for permission?"
- "Are you building leaders or followers right now?"
- "What decision could you hand off today that you're currently making for them?"
- "What would give your team enough clarity to act without you?"

## The Advantage — Patrick Lencioni
**Drives: Drives Clarity & Purpose**

**Core premise:** The single greatest advantage any organization can achieve is organizational health — minimal politics, minimal confusion, high morale, high productivity. And the foundation of organizational health is clarity: leaders who are aligned on why the organization exists, where it's going, and what matters most right now.

**Key frameworks to use in coaching:**
- **The Six Critical Questions** — Leaders must answer these with alignment:
  1. Why do we exist?
  2. How do we behave? (values)
  3. What do we do?
  4. How will we succeed?
  5. What is most important right now?
  6. Who must do what?
  - Use: "Could your team answer question #5 right now — what is most important right now — without guessing? If not, that's the gap."
- **Over-communicate clarity** — Leaders think they've communicated something clearly when they've said it once. The reality: people need to hear something 7 times before they internalize it.
  - Use: "Have you said this once, or have you made it impossible for your team to forget?"
- **The Rallying Cry** — The single most important priority right now. One thing. Not five.
  - Use: "If your team could only focus on one thing for the next 90 days, what would it be? Are they clear on that?"
- **Purpose before goals** — People don't get out of bed for targets. They get out of bed for meaning. Connect every priority to the why.

**Coaching language to use:**
- "Can your team answer: what is most important right now? Without asking you?"
- "Have you connected this work to a purpose, or just a metric?"
- "Is your team aligned on where you're going, or just marching in different directions?"
- "What's your rallying cry for the next 90 days?"

## The Servant Leader — Ken Blanchard
**Drives: Practices Courageous Stewardship**

**Core premise:** True leadership is not about the leader — it's about the people being led. Servant leadership inverts the typical organizational pyramid: instead of leading for the sake of power, status, or results, the servant leader leads in service of the growth, wellbeing, and flourishing of their people and organization. This requires both vision and humility.

**Key frameworks to use in coaching:**
- **Servant Leadership as Stewardship** — The leader holds the culture, the people, and the mission in trust. They are caretakers, not owners.
  - Use: "You're the steward of this team and this culture. What does protecting them look like, especially when it's hard?"
- **Vision-first Leadership** — Servant leaders don't just follow the team's wishes. They hold a clear, compelling vision and lead toward it with conviction — even when it's unpopular.
  - Use: "What are you unwilling to compromise on, no matter what the short-term pressure looks like?"
- **Ego vs. Service** — The biggest obstacle to servant leadership is ego. Blanchard identifies two kinds of ego failure: false humility (seeking to be liked) and arrogance (needing to be right).
  - Use: "Are you making this decision to serve your people or to protect yourself?"
- **The SERVE acronym:**
  - *See the future* — cast clear vision
  - *Engage and develop others* — grow people
  - *Reinvent continuously* — stay adaptive
  - *Value results and relationships* — both matter
  - *Embody the values* — walk the talk
- **Courageous decisions** — Real stewardship sometimes means making unpopular calls to protect long-term health over short-term comfort.
  - Use: "What's the decision that serves your people and the mission, even if it's not the popular one?"

**Coaching language to use:**
- "You're the steward of this team. What does protecting them look like right now?"
- "Is this decision serving your people, or is it serving your comfort?"
- "What values are you unwilling to compromise on, even under pressure?"
- "What would a servant leader do here, even if it's hard?"
